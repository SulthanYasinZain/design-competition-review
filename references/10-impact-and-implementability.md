# Impact, Stakeholder Value, and Implementability Audit

## 1. Purpose

Assess whether the proposed design could create meaningful value and whether the team has explained a credible pathway for that value to be delivered. This is especially important when the official rubric mentions “impact”, “industry partner”, “implementation”, “adoption”, “feasibility”, or “value proposition”.

The evaluator must not confuse these concepts:

- **Problem significance:** the problem matters.
- **Potential impact:** the solution could improve an outcome.
- **User value:** a person or organization would benefit.
- **Design feasibility:** the proposed experience can be designed and tested.
- **Implementation feasibility:** the system/service could be built and operated.
- **Adoption evidence:** real users or organizations show willingness to use it.
- **Demonstrated impact:** measured positive change after real use.

A UI/UX competition typically asks for a design and a plausible concept, not necessarily demonstrated real-world impact. Follow the exact rubric.

## 2. Stakeholder value proposition

For each stakeholder, identify:

- role and goals;
- current pain point;
- value delivered by the design;
- action the stakeholder must perform;
- benefit/cost/trade-off;
- information required;
- trust and adoption barrier;
- whether the stakeholder interface is included in the prototype or only conceptual.

Do not describe a survey respondent's positive opinion as a formal adoption commitment.

## 3. Service blueprint

For multi-party services, map:

- user actions;
- visible interface touchpoints;
- backstage processes;
- staff/partner actions;
- data exchanges;
- systems/tools needed;
- handoffs and response times;
- consent/permissions;
- failure points and recovery.

A prototype may show the frontstage flow while the blueprint explains the proposed service. This is appropriate for UI/UX work, provided the submission distinguishes the designed experience from an operating service.

## 4. Implementation dependencies

Identify dependencies such as:

- data source and content ownership;
- verification and update process;
- partner organizations;
- staff responsibilities;
- APIs and external systems;
- authentication and permission model;
- moderation and safety;
- accessibility testing;
- privacy/security controls;
- legal/regulatory constraints;
- funding and maintenance;
- onboarding and support;
- user acquisition and retention.

For each dependency, label:

- available;
- proposed;
- assumed;
- required partner;
- unresolved risk;
- outside current scope.

## 5. Industry/community partner evidence

If the rubric mentions an industry partner, inspect whether the team has:

- used the partner's actual problem statement;
- interviewed or consulted relevant staff;
- validated workflows with the partner;
- received feedback on the proposed design;
- documented willingness to explore a pilot;
- identified an owner for operational tasks;
- considered partner constraints and incentives.

Do not require a signed contract unless the brief requires it. A genuine meeting note or letter of interest may be stronger than a vague claim of “collaboration”. Do not fabricate or imply commitments that do not exist.

If there is no direct partner access, suggest alternatives:

- publicly documented process analysis;
- interviews with practitioners;
- expert review;
- a clearly stated pilot proposal;
- a service blueprint;
- explicit assumptions and validation plan.

These alternatives strengthen a design-stage case but are not equivalent to an actual partner commitment.

## 6. Impact logic model

Build a causal chain:

`Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Short-term outcomes → Longer-term outcomes → Impact`.

Example structure (project-specific data must be supplied):

- Input: design, verified data, organization participation.
- Activity: provide matching/filtering or support workflow.
- Output: users view information and submit requests.
- Short-term outcome: users make better-informed decisions or reduce task effort.
- Longer-term outcome: improved service access or coordination.
- Impact: broader social or organizational benefit.

Ask what assumptions connect each stage. A prototype interaction does not itself prove the longer-term outcome.

## 7. Measurable impact indicators

Choose indicators aligned with the user outcome, such as:

- task time or effort;
- accuracy of decisions;
- rate of successful access to service;
- number of verified listings;
- response time;
- request completion;
- user confidence or comprehension;
- reduction in unnecessary steps;
- adoption/return use;
- partner process efficiency;
- user-reported experience.

For each metric define baseline, numerator/denominator, data source, timeframe, owner, and privacy consideration. Do not claim impact without baseline and measurement.

## 8. Feasibility and risk

Evaluate:

- technical feasibility at the level expected by the rubric;
- operational feasibility;
- organizational readiness;
- data quality;
- privacy/security;
- accessibility maintenance;
- cost/effort;
- dependencies on external parties;
- failure modes;
- governance and accountability.

For a concept-stage project, identify what must be validated next rather than demanding impossible certainty.

## 9. Roadmap quality

A roadmap should contain more than feature names and quarters. For each phase include:

- objective;
- key activities;
- deliverables;
- dependencies;
- success criteria;
- assumptions/risks;
- responsible stakeholder where known.

If no delivery commitment exists, label dates as indicative. Do not present a speculative roadmap as an approved implementation schedule.

## 10. Required output

Produce:

1. stakeholder value matrix;
2. service blueprint or operational flow;
3. implementation dependency map;
4. partner evidence status;
5. impact logic model;
6. KPI definitions;
7. feasibility/risk register;
8. roadmap critique;
9. top actions that strengthen impact without requiring a production build.
