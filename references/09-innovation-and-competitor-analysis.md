# Innovation, Differentiation, and Competitor Analysis

## 1. Purpose

Determine whether the proposed solution offers a meaningful improvement for a defined user and whether its differentiation is supported by evidence. Novelty is not the same as adding AI, gamification, many features, or a new visual style.

## 2. Define the comparison set

Identify:

- direct competitors solving the same user task;
- indirect competitors or workarounds;
- substitutes (manual processes, offline services, spreadsheets, messaging groups, paper forms);
- adjacent services that solve part of the journey;
- existing internal tools used by the industry partner, if relevant.

Explain why each competitor belongs in the comparison. Do not compare a narrow specialist tool with an entire general ecosystem without clarifying the difference.

## 3. Competitor research protocol

For each competitor:

1. Identify the official product/service and target user.
2. Inspect current official website, app listing, help center, demo, or product documentation.
3. Record date accessed and evidence link.
4. Identify what is available, where, and to whom.
5. Distinguish public product features from partner-only services, programs, and manual delivery.
6. Note unknown or unverified features.
7. Avoid assuming absence from a webpage means non-existence.

## 4. Feature comparison rules

Use evidence-sensitive labels:

- `Yes — verified`;
- `Partial / different workflow`;
- `Not identified in reviewed public materials`;
- `Unknown`;
- `Not applicable`.

For every row with a yes/no-like judgment, include a source or audit note. A matrix of green checks and red crosses is visually clear but can be misleading if not supported.

Compare user outcomes, not just feature names. Two products may have features with similar labels but different scope or quality.

## 5. Differentiation quality

A defensible differentiator has:

- a clearly defined user segment;
- a meaningful unmet need;
- a comparison with current alternatives;
- a specific improvement in the user's experience;
- a reason the improvement matters;
- a feasible way to deliver it;
- evidence or a plan to validate it.

Ask:

- Is the feature genuinely different or simply renamed?
- Is it different in workflow, data, service, accessibility, or user outcome?
- Does the difference matter to the target user?
- Can the team demonstrate it in the prototype?
- Does the solution integrate existing value more effectively?
- Could an established competitor add the same feature easily?
- What would make the proposed experience hard to replicate: trusted data, partnerships, service processes, user trust, or a specific interaction model?

## 6. Innovation without technology decoration

Do not award innovation points merely because the proposal includes AI, blockchain, AR, speech recognition, personalization, or automation.

For each technology claim ask:

- What user problem does it solve?
- Why is this technology needed instead of a simpler approach?
- What data does it require?
- What happens when it fails?
- What are privacy, safety, bias, or accessibility risks?
- Is the technology in the prototype, a concept, or a future roadmap item?
- Is the team claiming novelty that already exists elsewhere?

A well-designed service integration can be innovative even without advanced technology. A sophisticated technology with no user justification can be weak innovation.

## 7. Competitor gap statement

A strong gap statement should not say “competitors do not have X” unless this is verified. Better structure:

“Reviewed alternatives provide A and B. In the sources reviewed, we found a gap in how [specific target user] can [specific task/outcome], particularly when [context]. The proposed design explores [specific improvement]. This differentiation requires further validation through [method].”

## 8. Market sizing and opportunity claims

If the project uses TAM/SAM/SOM or adoption estimates, verify:

- source of population/market data;
- definition of the target segment;
- calculation method;
- conversion assumptions;
- geographic scope;
- timeframe;
- overlap/double-counting;
- whether the estimate represents people, organizations, transactions, or revenue.

Do not accept a large market number without traceable arithmetic. Avoid describing a broad population as an addressable market if only a small segment can access the proposed service.

## 9. Required output

Produce:

- competitor set and rationale;
- dated evidence ledger;
- feature comparison matrix with confidence labels;
- unmet-need synthesis;
- differentiation statement;
- unsupported novelty claims;
- market-sizing audit if relevant;
- recommended validation or positioning changes.
