# Scoring Calibration and Final-Round Readiness

## 1. Purpose

Estimate performance against the actual rubric without claiming to know the judges' internal scoring. Help the team prioritize improvements that are likely to matter for qualification.

## 2. Score from evidence, not effort

Judges usually score what they can observe in the submitted material and presentation. Do not award points because:

- the team worked many hours;
- the team intended to do research;
- a planned feature is impressive;
- the prototype has many screens;
- the report is long;
- the team has a high SUS score without context;
- the project has an important social mission.

Credit the evidence that is visible, relevant, and convincing.

## 3. Criterion scoring rubric

Use the official score maximum. Within each criterion, assess:

- requirement alignment;
- evidence completeness;
- evidence credibility;
- execution quality;
- relevance to intended users;
- communication clarity;
- limitations and uncertainty.

If the official rubric has no detailed anchors, use a transparent internal scale only as an estimation aid:

- **0–20% of criterion:** absent, contradictory, or irrelevant evidence;
- **21–40%:** weak evidence; major gaps;
- **41–60%:** partially demonstrated; visible but incomplete;
- **61–80%:** good evidence; minor-to-moderate weaknesses;
- **81–95%:** very strong evidence; only limited gaps;
- **96–100%:** exceptional and comprehensively substantiated against the criterion.

These are internal calibration bands, not official judge standards. Do not present them as part of the competition rubric.

## 4. Score ranges and confidence

Use a range when judge interpretation is uncertain. Example:

`UX: 19–22/30 (medium confidence)`.

Explain what supports the lower/upper bound. Confidence levels:

- **High:** rubric is specific, evidence is directly inspectable, and scoring anchors are clear.
- **Medium:** evidence is inspectable but rubric interpretation or sample validity is uncertain.
- **Low:** important files, prototype access, test data, or judge guidance are missing.

Avoid decimal precision unless the official rubric or a verified formula requires it.

## 5. Judge inference versus fact

Use labels:

- `Observed issue`: directly visible in the submission.
- `Rubric risk`: likely to affect a described criterion.
- `Possible judge concern`: plausible but unconfirmed.
- `Known judge feedback`: explicitly provided by the organizer/judge.

Never write “the judges deducted points because…” unless judge feedback says that. Use “this may have contributed to a lower score” when reasoning from evidence.

## 6. Relative and absolute scoring

A score can be defensible yet insufficient to qualify. Qualification depends on:

- available finalist slots;
- competing submissions;
- judging calibration;
- possible pitch/demo stages;
- minimum thresholds or tie-breakers;
- other round-specific factors.

Do not infer ranking fairness from one score sheet. To analyze ranking, obtain the qualification cutoff, comparative scores, or official rank information if available.

BIBO is a useful caution: the project owner reported that its report score was among the top two while its final placing was third. The available evidence does not establish why. The evaluator must not infer a pitch or judge-specific cause.

## 7. Prioritization model

Prioritize changes using:

`Priority = rubric impact × evidence gap × likelihood of correction ÷ effort`.

This is a decision aid, not a mathematically validated formula. Rate each input qualitatively or on a transparent scale. Give priority to:

1. eligibility/submission blockers;
2. high-weight rubric weaknesses;
3. unsupported or contradictory claims;
4. central user-flow failures;
5. accessibility barriers relevant to target users;
6. lack of validation for core decisions;
7. impact/feasibility uncertainty where rubric rewards it;
8. visual polish.

Do not polish shadows while a core flow is broken or the research claim is unsupported.

## 8. Final-round readiness gates

A project should pass the following gates before submission:

### Gate A — Compliance

Every mandatory deliverable and rule has evidence. No unresolved P0 issue.

### Gate B — Problem credibility

The problem is clear, relevant to the brief, and supported by appropriately framed evidence.

### Gate C — User grounding

Primary user segments are defined; research limitations are explicit; persona/journey claims are traceable.

### Gate D — UX coherence

Primary flow is understandable, testable, and consistent across diagrams, screens, and report.

### Gate E — Accessibility credibility

Accessibility decisions are relevant to the target user, claims are calibrated to evidence, and untested areas are identified.

### Gate F — UI quality

Visual hierarchy and consistency are strong; critical states and responsive behavior are considered.

### Gate G — Prototype proof

The prototype demonstrates the primary scenario without broken interactions or misleading service claims.

### Gate H — Validation

Test methods, participants, metrics, findings, and iteration evidence are reported accurately.

### Gate I — Differentiation

Competitor claims are evidenced; the project articulates a meaningful user benefit.

### Gate J — Impact and feasibility

The value proposition and service model are credible at the level requested by the rubric. Dependencies and risks are explicit.

### Gate K — Communication

Report, pitch, and demo make the same claims and show the same feature scope.

## 9. What a high-readiness score means

A high internal readiness score means the submission is complete, coherent, evidence-backed, and defensible. It does not guarantee qualification. A perfect internal review is not a prediction of 100/100 because judges may weigh dimensions differently or compare the project against a stronger field.

## 10. Required output

Provide:

- official rubric scorecard;
- score range and confidence;
- evidence supporting each score;
- likely deduction areas;
- P0–P3 prioritized actions;
- readiness gate status;
- explicit uncertainty about qualification/rank.
