# Competition Brief, Guidebook, and Rubric Audit

## 1. Purpose

This module ensures that the evaluator reviews the project against the actual competition rather than against a generic definition of a “good app”. Different competitions reward different things: user research, visual execution, technical feasibility, social impact, partner relevance, innovation, or pitch quality. The evaluator must discover the competition's actual priorities before judging.

## 2. Source hierarchy

Use this order of authority unless the organizer explicitly states otherwise:

1. Latest written clarification from the competition organizer.
2. Official guidebook/rulebook and official problem brief.
3. Official scoring rubric and judging descriptions.
4. Official submission portal instructions and announcement.
5. Official organizer FAQs or public clarifications.
6. Team interpretation and informal advice.
7. General competition conventions and professional practice.

If two official documents conflict, do not choose silently. Record the discrepancy and identify which document appears newer or more specific. Ask for organizer clarification where the issue could affect eligibility or deliverables.

## 3. Extract the competition contract

Create a structured record with the following fields:

### 3.1 Identity

- Competition name and year.
- Category/track.
- Main theme.
- Subtheme.
- Named industry or community partner, if any.
- Problem statement or challenge statement.
- Intended users and beneficiary groups.
- Required scope or constraints.

### 3.2 Eligibility and administration

- Eligible participants and team size.
- Institution/region/age restrictions.
- Registration and submission deadlines with time zone.
- Required registration data.
- Required consent or declaration forms.
- Plagiarism, AI-use, originality, or intellectual-property rules.
- Disqualification conditions.

### 3.3 Deliverables

Record each separately:

- proposal/report;
- presentation deck;
- prototype link;
- video pitch;
- live demonstration;
- appendix/evidence;
- source code or technical artifact, only if required;
- file format and maximum size;
- page, slide, and duration limits;
- file naming conventions;
- access requirements for links;
- whether cover, table of contents, references, and appendices count toward page limits.

Never infer page-count exclusions from convention. Quote the exact rule or mark it for organizer confirmation.

### 3.4 Judging criteria

For each criterion capture:

- exact official name;
- exact description;
- maximum points;
- subcriteria explicitly mentioned;
- whether the description includes implementation, social impact, partner alignment, accessibility, or communication;
- any criterion-specific eligibility threshold or minimum score.

Do not replace a broad criterion with your own interpretation without showing the mapping. For example, “Impact” may refer to social outcomes, industry-partner adoption, feasibility, business value, or some combination.

## 4. Compliance matrix

Use one row per mandatory requirement. Recommended statuses:

- `PASS` — visible evidence fully meets the requirement.
- `PARTIAL` — requirement is present but incomplete or ambiguous.
- `MISSING` — mandatory item is absent from the submission.
- `UNCLEAR` — available documents do not allow verification.
- `N/A` — not applicable under the official rules.

For every row record a direct reference: guidebook page/section and submission page/slide/frame. Do not use an unexplained “yes” or “no”.

## 5. Brief-to-design alignment

A theme-compliant title is not sufficient. Evaluate whether the problem, users, features, interactions, and evidence align with the brief.

Ask:

1. Does the problem actually belong to the stated theme/subtheme?
2. Does the project address the problem named by the organizer, rather than a loosely related issue?
3. Is the intended user group consistent with the problem brief?
4. Are all mandatory needs addressed, explicitly deferred, or justified as out of scope?
5. Does the prototype show the most important parts of the solution?
6. Does the proposal explain why this solution is appropriate for the industry/community partner?
7. Are there features that distract from the stated brief?
8. Are claims of impact or implementation aligned with the partner's actual capabilities and responsibilities?

## 6. Rubric interpretation method

For each criterion, produce four layers:

### Layer A — Official meaning

Quote or accurately paraphrase the rubric. Do not smuggle in unlisted expectations.

### Layer B — Observable evidence

Identify what a judge can actually see in the submission: report text, research table, prototype interaction, test results, user feedback, service blueprint, partner evidence, and pitch demonstration.

### Layer C — Quality judgment

Evaluate the evidence's depth, validity, clarity, and relevance. The presence of a slide is not equivalent to full credit.

### Layer D — Uncertainty

Identify what cannot be known without judge comments, access to the actual test, hidden scoring guidance, or competitor submissions.

## 7. Detecting hidden deliverable risks

Check specifically for:

- prototype links requiring access permission;
- QR codes that are too small or lead to an outdated prototype;
- screenshots from an earlier design version;
- broken hyperlinks;
- slide decks that exceed video/pitch time;
- appendices that contradict the main report;
- captions that do not match figures;
- references that do not support adjacent claims;
- required declaration forms omitted;
- incorrect page-limit calculations;
- different project names, team names, or feature sets across documents;
- unsupported statistics or fabricated/ambiguous survey findings;
- private test data or personal information exposed in appendices.

## 8. When the brief is vague

Do not fill ambiguity with arbitrary rules. Create an `Assumption Register` with:

- ambiguity;
- possible interpretations;
- evidence from official wording;
- impact if the assumption is wrong;
- recommended conservative response;
- whether organizer clarification is needed.

If no clarification is available, advise the team to satisfy the stricter plausible interpretation only when that does not contradict another rule.

## 9. Output expected from this module

1. One-page brief summary.
2. Exact rubric table with point weights.
3. Compliance matrix with references and status.
4. List of brief-alignment gaps.
5. Assumption register.
6. Mandatory pre-submission actions.

## 10. Common evaluator errors to avoid

- Scoring before reading the guidebook.
- Assuming every UI/UX competition requires a production application.
- Assuming a page limit includes or excludes appendices without checking.
- Treating generic UX best practices as official requirements.
- Giving a high score because every section heading exists.
- Giving a low score because a non-required deliverable is absent.
- Ignoring industry-partner requirements when they are explicitly present in the rubric.
- Treating the theme name as proof of theme alignment.
