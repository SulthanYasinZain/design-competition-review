# Report, Pitch, Demo, and Submission Audit

## 1. Purpose

Ensure the design work is communicated clearly, accurately, and within the competition constraints. Good design can be under-credited when the report hides its logic, the deck is unreadable, the demo breaks, or the team overstates what the prototype does.

## 2. Report narrative

A strong report usually makes the logic easy to follow:

1. competition context and problem;
2. target users and stakeholders;
3. research method and findings;
4. user needs and problem framing;
5. solution concept and feature priorities;
6. UX architecture and journey;
7. wireframes and design system;
8. prototype and interaction decisions;
9. usability/accessibility testing;
10. competitor/differentiation analysis;
11. impact and implementation pathway;
12. limitations, next steps, and conclusion.

Follow the required structure from the guidebook. Do not insert optional sections that push mandatory content outside the page limit.

## 3. Report evidence and writing audit

Check:

- every statistic has a supporting source;
- every research claim points to the relevant method/result;
- figure captions match the figures;
- numbering is consistent;
- terminology is consistent across sections;
- the same feature list appears in report, prototype, and pitch;
- future features are labelled as future;
- prototype simulation is not described as a live service;
- limitations are acknowledged;
- conclusion reflects actual evidence;
- references are complete and accessible;
- appendices do not contradict the main body;
- no placeholders, comments, template text, or unfinished notes remain.

Do not rewrite content merely to sound academic if it makes the argument less clear. Use concise formal language.

## 4. Slide deck structure

Adapt to the exact required slide count and time. A useful competition narrative often includes:

- project identity;
- problem and why it matters;
- target user and research insight;
- opportunity/gap;
- solution and value proposition;
- user flow;
- key features;
- prototype demo;
- validation and iteration;
- impact/roadmap if time permits;
- conclusion.

Do not force every report section into the video. If the time limit is short, prioritize the problem, user evidence, solution, key flow, and strongest proof. Keep backup details in the report or appendix if the competition allows it.

## 5. Slide-level quality

For each slide ask:

- What is the one message the audience should remember?
- Can it be understood in 3–5 seconds?
- Is the evidence legible on a projected screen or mobile video?
- Is the title a conclusion/message rather than a vague label?
- Are screenshots large enough?
- Is there a clear visual hierarchy?
- Does the slide add new information rather than repeat the previous slide?
- Are all numbers and claims sourced?

## 6. Pitch timing

Allocate time intentionally. Include:

- opening/problem context;
- research insight;
- proposed solution;
- feature explanation;
- demo;
- evidence/validation;
- closing value proposition.

Reserve time for transitions and loading the prototype. Do not allocate every second to spoken content. Practice to the actual limit, not a guessed duration.

## 7. Demo planning

Choose a single primary persona and scenario. The demo should show the most important problem-solving flow, not every screen.

A demo script should include:

- user context;
- task goal;
- action on screen;
- design decision worth noticing;
- outcome;
- why the outcome matters.

Test the demo on the actual device, screen recording setup, network, and presentation environment. Prepare a backup recording and screenshots when permitted.

## 8. Judge question preparation

Prepare short, evidence-backed answers to:

- Why did you choose this problem?
- Who did you research and why?
- What is the most important insight?
- Why is this feature necessary?
- How is the solution different from alternatives?
- How did you test it?
- Who tested the prototype?
- What does the SUS/task-success result actually prove?
- What accessibility needs are supported and which remain untested?
- What is simulated versus implemented?
- Who operates the service?
- What is the biggest risk?
- What would you build or validate next?
- What would you remove if development time were cut in half?

Answers should be direct and honest. “We have not validated that yet” is better than inventing evidence.

## 9. Final file and link audit

Verify:

- exact required file type;
- page/slide limit interpretation;
- correct file name;
- correct team/project name;
- correct category and theme;
- working prototype link and permissions;
- working QR codes;
- citations and references;
- captions and page numbers;
- no empty pages caused by layout breaks;
- no unfinished placeholders;
- no hidden comments or editing marks;
- video/audio clarity;
- correct duration;
- submission portal confirmation.

## 10. Required output

Produce:

- report narrative audit;
- slide-by-slide message audit;
- pitch timing plan;
- demo path and backup plan;
- judge Q&A bank;
- submission compliance checklist;
- P0/P1 issues before upload.
